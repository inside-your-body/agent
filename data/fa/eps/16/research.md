Timings vary between sources, so I flag the conflicts below. The ⭐ items are the most surprising for a video.

## Minutes to hours

1. **20 minutes: heart rate and blood pressure drop.** Nicotine-driven spikes start to ease.
 - WHO lists heart rate and blood pressure dropping within 20 minutes.
 - The AHA describes this as blood pressure and heart rate dropping from the nicotine-induced spikes.
 - Sources: https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation and https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-lifestyle/quit-smoking-tobacco/the-benefits-of-quitting-smoking-now
 - Viewer moment: you can check your own pulse. The NHS says your pulse will already be starting to return to normal.

2. **⭐ 2 hours: half the nicotine is already gone.** Nicotine has a half-life of around 2 hours, so about half is removed by then.
 - That short half-life is why the effects go away quickly and people soon feel they need another dose. It explains the "need one after a meal or a coffee" feeling.
 - Source: https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/322526

3. **⭐ 8 hours: the carbon monoxide (CO) story.**
 - The NHS says oxygen levels are recovering and the carbon monoxide level in your blood has reduced by half.
 - Mechanism: CO from smoke binds to hemoglobin in place of oxygen, so removing it lets blood carry more oxygen to your organs.
 - Source: https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/quit-smoking/
 - Fun comparison: Healthline notes carbon monoxide is the same dangerous fume that comes from car exhaust. Source: https://www.healthline.com/health/effects-of-quitting-smoking

4. **12 hours: CO is back to normal (WHO).** WHO says the carbon monoxide level in your blood drops to normal.
 - ⚠️ Conflict: the NHS says it reaches non-smoker level at 48 hours, and the American Lung Association says 12–24 hours. Say "within 12–48 hours depending on the source."
 - Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation

## Days

5. **24 hours: heart attack risk begins to fall.** The American Lung Association lists carbon monoxide normal at 12–24 hours and heart attack risk significantly reduced.
 - Source: https://www.lung.org/quit-smoking/i-want-to-quit/benefits-of-quitting
 - Context: WebMD says a pack-a-day smoker is twice as likely to have a heart attack as a nonsmoker. Source: https://www.webmd.com/smoking-cessation/what-happens-body-quit-smoking

6. **⭐ 24–72 hours: cilia wake up, and the cough gets worse.** Smoke paralyzes and destroys some of the tiny hair-like cilia in the airways, so the remaining ones struggle to clear mucus.
 - After quitting, cilia recover and move mucus out, so you may cough more than usual, for a few weeks or up to a year.
 - Source: https://www.beaconhealthsystem.org/library/faqs/coughing-more-after-quitting-smoking-whats-the-deal?content_id=FAQ-20057818
 - Myth-buster: more coughing is a sign of healing, not damage. Caveat from the same source: if the cough lasts longer than a month, check with a health care provider.

7. **48 hours: taste and smell improve.** NHS Inform says you'll notice improvements in breathing and in taste and smell a few days after stopping.
 - Healthline attributes this to damaged nerve endings beginning to regrow.
 - Sources: https://www.nhsinform.scot/healthy-living/stopping-smoking/when-you-stop/myths-around-stopping-smoking/ and https://www.healthline.com/health/effects-of-quitting-smoking
 - Everyday moment: food suddenly tastes stronger.

8. **72 hours: bronchial tubes relax.** The NHS says breathing feels easier because the bronchial tubes have started to relax, and energy increases.
 - Source: https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/quit-smoking/

9. **⭐ Days 2–3: the brain is at its worst.** Withdrawal begins 4 to 24 hours after the last dose and peaks on the second or third day.
 - A PubMed review agrees: symptoms peak on approximately the 3rd day and taper off over the following 3–4 weeks.
 - Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25638335/ and https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21587-nicotine-withdrawal
 - Mechanism: nicotine binds to brain receptors and triggers dopamine release.
 - Regular smoking upregulates these nicotinic receptors, so the brain grows more of them. With no nicotine, those upregulated receptors are suddenly unstimulated.
 - Source for the receptor mechanism (secondary): https://getburnout.app/articles/science/nicotine-withdrawal-brain-chemistry
 - Narrative hook: day 3 is the "top of the mountain", not a random bad day.

10. **Cravings are short.** Individual cravings last about 3–5 minutes at their sharpest. This is from a secondary source, so treat it as indicative. Source: https://getburnout.app/articles/quit/nicotine-withdrawal-symptoms

## Weeks to months

11. **2–12 weeks: circulation and lung function improve.** WHO says your circulation improves and your lung function increases.
 - The NHS says blood pumps through to your heart and muscles much better.
 - Everyday situation: climbing stairs gets easier. WebMD says you can exercise without getting as winded.
 - Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation

12. **3–9 months: lung function up by as much as 10%.** The NHS says coughs, wheezing and breathing problems improve as lung function increases by up to 10%. WHO gives the window as 1–9 months for coughing and shortness of breath to decrease.
 - Source: https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/quit-smoking/
 - ⚠️ Some blogs claim a "30% lung function" gain at 1–3 months. I could not trace that to WHO, CDC or the NHS, so I'd avoid it.

## Years

13. **1 year: heart disease risk is halved (the NHS/WHO figure).** WHO says your risk of coronary heart disease is about half that of a smoker's.
 - ⚠️ Conflict: the AHA says three to six years for a 50% reduction, and a secondary source notes the 2020 Surgeon General's report estimates 4–5 years.
 - The honest script line is "about half within one to a few years."
 - Sources: https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation and https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-lifestyle/quit-smoking-tobacco/the-benefits-of-quitting-smoking-now

14. **5–15 years: stroke risk falls to a nonsmoker's.** WHO says stroke risk is reduced to that of a nonsmoker 5 to 15 years after quitting. Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation

15. **10 years: lung cancer risk is halved.** WHO says the risk of lung cancer falls to about half that of a smoker, and the risk of cancers of the mouth, throat, esophagus, bladder, cervix and pancreas decreases. Source: same WHO page.

16. **15 years: heart disease risk is a nonsmoker's.** WHO says the risk of coronary heart disease is that of a nonsmoker's. The AHA says almost the same as a non-smoker's.

## Cell-level surprises

17. **⭐ Healthy cells take back the airway lining.** A 2020 *Nature* study (Wellcome Sanger Institute and UCL) sequenced airway cells from 16 people.
 - More than 9 out of 10 lung cells in current smokers had up to 10,000 extra genetic changes versus non-smokers.
 - In ex-smokers, four times more healthy cells than in people who still smoked, up to 40% of total lung cells.
 - The study author says damaged cells are not repaired; they are replaced by healthy cells that escaped the damage.
 - Sources: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2020/jan/protective-cells-could-cut-risk-lung-cancer-ex-smokers and https://www.uclhospitals.brc.nihr.ac.uk/news/protective-cells-could-cut-risk-lung-cancer-ex-smokers
 - Caveat from the researchers: smoking also causes deeper damage that can lead to emphysema.

18. **⭐ It's never too late.** WHO gives life-expectancy gains from quitting at different ages: about 10 years at 30, 9 at 40, 6 at 50 and 3 at 60.
 - After a heart attack, quitting reduces the chance of another by 50%.
 - Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation

## Myths and everyday situations

- **Myth: "A cigarette relaxes me."** NHS Inform says nicotine returns to the level your brain is used to, and the physical stress on your body is actually increasing. Source: https://www.nhsinform.scot/healthy-living/stopping-smoking/when-you-stop/myths-around-stopping-smoking/
- **Myth: "Quitting makes you gain weight."** NHS Inform says nicotine suppresses appetite and speeds calorie burning, but if you keep to a healthy diet it's unlikely you'll gain weight. Same source.
- **Myth: "Just one reward cigarette is fine."** The mechanism is that it refills the receptors. This comes from a hypnosis-app blog, so use it as a framing idea only. Source: https://metashifts.com/first-72-hours-quit-smoking-timeline-craving-hypnosis/
- **Vaping caveat:** the NHS says e-cigarettes don't produce tar or carbon monoxide, so the CO milestones don't map onto vaping. Source: https://addiction-recovery.app/blog/quit-smoking-timeline/
- **Test detail:** the metabolite cotinine has a half-life of roughly 16 to 18 hours (the figures given range from 7 to 40 hours), which is why tests look for it, not nicotine. Source: https://www.takeroon.com/blog/how-long-does-nicotine-stay-in-your-system
