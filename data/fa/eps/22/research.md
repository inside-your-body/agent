I'll run several searches in parallel to cover the timeline from seconds to months.Here is a timeline-ordered fact list. Most numbers come from cold-water immersion studies, not showers; I note that where it matters.

**SECONDS (0–90 s): the cold shock**
1. **The gasp is a reflex.** Skin cooling triggers it before you choose it. The response is stimulated by a rapid fall in skin temperature and includes tachycardia, a reflex inspiratory gasp, then uncontrollable hyperventilation. In one study the gasp occurred within the first 10 s of immersion. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4580772/
2. **Blood vessels clamp shut.** Skin cold receptors signal the brain and trigger the sympathetic "fight or flight" system. Vessels near the skin clamp shut, pushing blood pressure up. One source (not a primary study) estimates a spike of 15–25 mmHg systolic within seconds. https://www.boots.com/healthhub/mental-health-support/cold-shower-benefits and https://www.cardilog.app/blog/cold-plunge-blood-pressure
3. **Breath-holding collapses (a surprising fact).** In cold water below about 15 °C, the ability to hold your breath drops from the normal 60–90 seconds to just a few seconds. That is why cold shock, not hypothermia, is the main killer in cold-water falls. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_shock_response (the Experimental Physiology review "Cold water immersion: kill or cure?" (2017) covers this in the primary literature)

**MINUTES (1–15 min): settling and the chemical surge**
4. **The shock is brief.** The initial response typically lasts less than 5 minutes. The gasp phase is mostly over in 2–3 minutes. In a shower you can simply step out. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_shock_response
5. **Norepinephrine surges, even from very short dips.** A 20-second whole-body dip in 4.4 °C water raised norepinephrine 200–300%. Norepinephrine and systolic blood pressure rise within 2–15 minutes of immersion, notably without substantial epinephrine elevation. https://www.foundmyfitness.com/episodes/cold-shock-norepinephrine and https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0317615 (PLOS ONE 2025 meta-analysis)

**HOURS (1–3 h): the dopamine and immune effects**
6. **The famous numbers are from an extreme protocol.** One hour of neck-deep immersion at 14 °C raised norepinephrine 530% and dopamine 250%. Energy expenditure rose 350%. Water at 32 °C and 20 °C did not trigger norepinephrine release. The study is Šrámek et al., Eur J Appl Physiol, 2000. https://www.foundmyfitness.com/topics/cold-exposure-therapy
7. **Myth check: the "2–3 hour dopamine high".** Claims of a two-hour post-plunge "dopamine high" go beyond what the study measured. The 530%/250% figures came from plasma sampled during a controlled hour in cold water, not from a shower. https://scienceblog.com/t-cold-immersion-plasma-noradrenaline-dopamine-two-hour-claim/
8. **Immersion also makes you urinate more.** In that same 14 °C study, diuresis rose 163%. This matches the everyday "need to pee after a cold dip" experience. https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/13/7107
9. **White blood cells jump.** In the J Appl Physiol 1999 study, total white cell counts rose significantly and stayed elevated at 2 h of cold exposure. The mechanism is thought to be norepinephrine-mediated mobilization of demarginated cells, meaning cells already in the body are flushed into the blood rather than newly made. https://journals.physiology.org/doi/pdf/10.1152/jappl.1999.87.2.699
10. **The immune picture is mixed.** A PLOS ONE meta-analysis found no significant effects on immune function immediately or 1 hour after cold-water immersion. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0317615

**DAYS (about 5–10 days): habituation**
11. **Your panic response shrinks fast.** Repeated cold immersions over several days reduce the magnitude of the cold shock response. One secondary summary reports about a 50% reduction in the gasp response after 5 consecutive days at 15 °C, but the primary abstract shows the effect is not uniform. The first-10-second gasp was similar between the first and seventh immersions, while breathing rate and volume fell. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4580772/
12. **Hormone reactions change with experience.** In regular winter swimmers, 24 h after a 4 °C dip, noradrenaline and adrenaline were significantly reduced and cortisol slightly increased compared with controls. https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/13/7107
13. **Metabolic change can start within 10 days.** After a 10-day cold acclimation period (mild cold, not showers), brown fat activity and non-shivering heat production rose. In type 2 diabetes patients, glucose infusion rate during the clamp rose from 15.1 to 21.4 ml/kg/min, a sign of better insulin sensitivity. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4580778/

**WEEKS (4–12 weeks): brown fat and metabolism**
14. **Brown fat grows.** In six cold-naïve men exposed to 10 °C for 2 h/day, 5 days/week for 4 weeks, brown fat volume rose 45% and its oxidative metabolism more than doubled. That was a cooling garment, so it is much harsher than a shower. (This is a patent-text summary; the original is Yoneshiro et al., 2013.) https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12496253
15. **Cold-activated brown fat improves insulin sensitivity.** In lean men, peripheral glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity rose 20% during cold-induced brown fat activation. https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/11/4226/4117435 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2017)
16. **A small weekly dose may be enough, per Søberg.** She reports that 11 minutes of cold water immersion per week, divided across 2–3 days, activated brown fat and increased insulin sensitivity in healthy subjects. This is an interview quote, so it should be checked against her paper. https://www.whoop.com/us/en/thelocker/podcast-207-dr-susanna-soberg-on-the-benefits-of-cold-therapy/

**MONTHS (about 3 months): sickness, mood, muscle**
17. **The best human shower trial is modest.** In a Dutch RCT of 3,018 adults, a hot-to-cold shower regimen cut sickness absence 29% (IRR 0.71, P = 0.003). But illness days showed no significant group effect. The authors suggest intensity rather than duration of symptoms is modulated. The 30-, 60- and 90-second groups did not differ significantly. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27631616/ (PLoS One 2016)
18. **Cold after lifting can cost you muscle.** A 2024 meta-analysis found post-exercise cold-water immersion may cause at least a small reduction in hypertrophy. A later umbrella review put the effect at small (cSMD = −0.22). Whole-body immersion showed no meaningful effect on strength gains (SMD −0.08), while limb-only immersion impaired them. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11235606/ and https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-026-01116-8

**Myths and safety for the script**
- **"Cold showers burn lots of fat."** The brown-fat effects came from hours of cooling per day (e.g. 2 h daily at 17 °C for 6 weeks), not a 2-minute shower. https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12496253
- **"It boosts immunity."** The 29% figure is self-reported work absence, not blinded, with no change in illness days. https://www.icy.ie/cold-showers-what-the-evidence-says
- **Heart risk.** Sudden cold can cause sharp changes in heart rate and blood pressure, which could raise risk for people with heart or circulatory conditions. https://www.boots.com/healthhub/mental-health-support/cold-shower-benefits
