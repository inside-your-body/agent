## PART 1: THE TIMELINE (minutes to hours)

**1. 0–10 min: the sugar load arrives**
- A single can of sugar-sweetened soda has up to 40 grams (about 10 teaspoons) of free sugars (WHO/PAHO).
- Coke's own figure is 39 grams in a 12 oz (355 ml) can.
- On WHO's stricter 5% level, that is a whole day's allowance in one drink. WHO suggests about 25 g (6 tsp) per day.
- 🔗 https://www.paho.org/en/news/4-3-2015-paho-and-who-urge-countries-reduce-sugar-consumption-among-adults-and-children

**2. 10–15 min: the gut floods the blood with glucose**
- Within the first 10 to 15 minutes, the intestines send sugar into the blood, spiking glucose.
- The pancreas releases insulin to move the sugar to the muscles for energy. But the soda contains much more sugar than the muscles need.
- 🔗 https://www.foxnews.com/health/doctors-explain-what-happens-to-your-body-after-drinking-soda

**3. ~20 min: insulin burst and the liver turns sugar into fat (popular infographic claim)**
- Blood sugar spikes, causing an insulin burst, and the liver turns any sugar it can into fat. This is the viral version.
- The real mechanism is de novo lipogenesis. In studies, modest consumption of fructose- or sucrose-sweetened drinks over several weeks increased hepatic fatty acid synthesis in healthy men. Over weeks, not 20 minutes.
- 🔗 https://fit.thequint.com/health-news/what-happens-in-an-hour-of-drinking-coca-cola-2 (viral claim)
- 🔗 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10629746/ (mechanism, PMC review)

**4. 5 minutes to 60 min: caffeine hits the brain**
- Caffeine is detectable in plasma 5 minutes after ingestion, with peak levels in 30–60 minutes (Medscape).
- A 12 oz cola has about 34 mg of caffeine, roughly a third of a cup of coffee. The infographic's "adenosine receptors blocked, so no drowsiness" is the standard mechanism for caffeine generally.
- 🔗 https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/821863-overview

**5. Hours: caffeine lingers long after the fizz is gone**
- After 3 to 5 hours, 50% of caffeine has been excreted. Medscape gives an average half-life of 5–8 hours in healthy, nonsmoking adults.
- A 4 pm cola still leaves caffeine in your system at bedtime. This is a good recognisable scenario for viewers.
- 🔗 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7468986/

**6. ~45–60 min: dopamine and the "sugar crash" (weak evidence)**
- The infographic says dopamine production rises, stimulating the pleasure centres, and an hour later a sugar crash begins, causing irritability and drowsiness.
- Treat the "comparable to heroin" line as a myth or exaggeration. It has no clinical backing, and Medical News Today repeats it only as a claim from the infographic.
- A better-grounded point: a Princeton study found that when hungry rats were fed a sugary solution, their brains released dopamine, a chemical that triggers motivation and reward, which can become addictive. That is an animal study.
- 🔗 https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/297600

**7. Kidneys: spilling sugar and water**
- The kidneys get rid of excess sugar through urine, so the body loses water. Along with caffeine's diuretic effect, this increases dehydration risk.
- Be careful here: at 34 mg, the diuretic effect of one can is modest.

## PART 2: TEETH (starts in seconds and builds over days)

**8. Coke's pH is about 2.4, and enamel starts dissolving at 5.5**
- Enamel erodes at pH 5.5, and Coca-Cola Classic has a pH of about 2.37.
- A lab study of soft drinks found pH values from 2.42 to 3.46, all below the critical pH of 5.5 for enamel demineralization.
- 🔗 https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0929664614001831
- 🔗 https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10834952 (patent background, cites the pH figure; lower-grade source)

**9. Two attacks at once: acid plus bacteria**
- Continuously sipping soda creates an acid bath for teeth, and bacteria on the teeth convert the sugar into acid.
- Dental erosion is the irreversible loss of dental structure from a chemical process, without microorganisms.
- Surprising detail: in the lab, increasing temperature, concentration and exposure time increased the erosion of dentine and enamel. Slow sipping is worse than drinking quickly.
- 🔗 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5771309
- 🔗 https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11767482_The_effect_of_pH_on_the_erosion_of_dentine_and_enamel_by_dietary_acids_in_vitro

**10. Saliva can partly repair the damage**
- Demineralisation from acid drinks may be reversible because saliva can remineralise the teeth. Constant sipping never gives saliva the chance.

## PART 3: WEEKS TO MONTHS

**11. Weeks: liver fat builds up**
- Developing fatty liver usually takes at least 8–24 weeks on a high-fructose diet, with more progressive disease on longer exposure.
- Giving sugary drinks for 6 months to humans increased liver fat, confirmed by magnetic resonance spectroscopy.
- Surprising: carbohydrate overfeeding for 3 weeks caused a greater than 10-fold larger relative change in liver fat (27%) than in body weight (2%). The liver fills up before the scale moves.
- The effect is reversible. In children with high fructose intake, liver fat fell from a median of 7.2% to 3.8% after just 9 days of fructose restriction.
- Caveat: some groups argue fructose may not raise NAFLD risk in short trials of 4 weeks or less that compare fructose to isocaloric diets.
- 🔗 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5893377/
- 🔗 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5663253/
- 🔗 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10629746/

**12. Weeks to months: liquid sugar bypasses "I'm full" signals**
- The rationale from the trial protocol: liquid carbohydrates are thought to be less satiating than solid ones, so calories from sugary drinks may not be offset by eating less at later meals.
- In the DRINK randomised trial (NEJM, 641 children), replacing one 250 mL can a day of sugary drink with a sugar-free one reduced weight gain by 1 kg in 18 months.
- Nuance: that trial found sugar-sweetened and sugar-free beverages produced similar satiety. The mechanism is still debated.
- 🔗 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22998340/
- 🔗 https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4957753/

**13. Two weeks: appetite hormone changes**
- In a trial of 131 adults, participants drank 3 sweetened beverages a day, supplying 25% of energy requirement as glucose, fructose, sucrose or HFCS, for 2 weeks. Researchers hypothesised that fructose's effect of lowering 24-h leptin concentrations may be a mechanism for increased energy intake.
- 🔗 https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7765993/

## PART 4: YEARS (population-level risk)

**14. Type 2 diabetes risk rises with each daily serving**
- A BMJ meta-analysis found 18% greater incidence of type 2 diabetes per serving a day, and 13% after adjusting for adiposity.
- A PubMed meta-analysis of 310,819 participants and 15,043 cases found 26% greater risk in the highest intake group (usually 1–2 servings a day).
- A 2026 review summarises it as a 13–30% rise in T2DM risk per daily serving.
- Surprising nuance: the form in which sugar is consumed appears more relevant to T2DM risk than the type of sugar itself. Drinks carry more risk than solid foods.
- 🔗 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4510779/
- 🔗 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20693348/
- 🔗 https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12904972/

**15. Bones: cola, specifically**
- The Framingham Osteoporosis Study (Am J Clin Nutr, 2006) found women with daily cola intake had 3.7% lower bone density at the femoral neck and 5.4% lower at Ward's area than those drinking less than one cola a month.
- Surprising: cola but not other carbonated soft drinks was associated with low BMD in women, and the finding was not seen in men. The study is observational, and the authors say additional research is needed to confirm these findings.
- 🔗 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17023723/

**16. Kidneys: phosphoric acid and stones**
- In a randomised trial among men with kidney stones, those who continued drinking phosphoric acid-containing soft drinks had higher stone recurrence than those who switched to citric acid beverages.
- A secondary source reports that drinking two or more colas a day was associated with 2.3 times higher chronic kidney disease risk. Verify against the original paper before using this number on air.
- 🔗 https://scienceinsights.org/is-phosphoric-acid-good-or-bad-for-your-health/ (secondary summary; find the primary trial and the Nurses' Health Study / ARIC papers before publishing)

## MYTHS AND CAVEATS TO MENTION IN THE VIDEO

- **"Phosphoric acid cuts the sweetness so you don't vomit."** This is an infographic claim: you don't vomit from the overwhelming sweetness because phosphoric acid cuts the flavour. It is not clinically established. Label it a viral claim.
- **"Coke is like heroin."** No clinical evidence, so treat it as a myth.
- **"One can ruins your health."** A doctor quoted in the Fox piece says an occasional soda is not going to have a significant impact on your health. The risks come from habit.
- **Diet cola isn't a free pass on the non-sugar issues.** In the Framingham data, diet cola and decaffeinated cola showed similar bone-density patterns. That points to phosphoric acid rather than sugar or caffeine.
- **Correlation vs causation:** most of the diabetes, bone and kidney data are observational cohorts, while the liver and weight data include some randomised trials.
