## Timeline: what happens when you never brush

**Seconds to minutes**
1. **Within minutes, a protein film coats the enamel.** This "pellicle" forms from saliva proteins and gives bacteria something to grip. It forms within minutes after tooth eruption, teeth cleaning or chewing. A patent filing says it reforms within about two hours after removal. (Secondary source.) https://www.eoperio.com.au/blog/dental-plaque
2. **Sugar triggers an acid attack within minutes.** Plaque pH falls rapidly below the critical value within 2-5 minutes of exposure to sucrose or glucose. The critical pH of 5.5 is the point below which enamel begins to dissolve, followed by slow recovery over 30-60 minutes. (University lecture notes.) https://codental.uobaghdad.edu.iq/wp-content/uploads/sites/14/uploads/0000000Lectures/5th/Pedo%20and%20Prevention/prevention%202017-2018/lect%2015%20prevention%20%205th%20%20grade%20%202018.pdf
3. **Plaque starts to regrow about 20-30 minutes after cleaning.** It re-forms within 20 to 30 minutes after brushing, as bacteria recolonise the pellicle. (Dental-clinic blog.) https://www.dentalhygienist.london/blog/why-plaque-builds-up-quickly-and-what-you-can-change-daily

**Hours**
4. **Pioneer bacteria attach within hours.** Single cells of mostly gram-positive cocci and rods from the normal oral flora colonize the surface within hours of pellicle formation. After several days, actinomycetes predominate and the polysaccharide matrix of a biofilm begins to develop. (Patent background.) https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7494980
5. **The biofilm shields itself.** The volume and structure of the biofilm protects the bacteria inside it, potentially reducing the effect of antimicrobials. Oxygen drops inside the plaque, which favours anaerobic bacteria. This is a good "city of microbes" visual. (Patent background.) https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11000710

**Days 1-3**
6. **Plaque starts hardening into tartar in 24-72 hours.** Minerals from saliva are deposited into the plaque, hardening it within 24 to 72 hours into calculus. Once formed, calculus cannot be removed by conventional brushing and flossing. The 24-72 hour figure is widely repeated but comes mostly from clinic blogs and patents. One source adds that noticeable tartar typically requires several days of undisturbed accumulation. https://www.dentalhygienist.london/blog/how-quickly-can-plaque-turn-into-dental-tartar
7. **Plaque doubles in about two days.** A patent filing says within about two days, if not removed, plaque approximately doubles in mass. It also notes over 700 species of bacteria reside in the human oral cavity. (Secondary source.) https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9681932
8. **The bacterial mix changes in three phases (Theilade 1966, J Periodontal Res).** In the first 2 days, gram-positive cocci and rods proliferate and about 30% gram-negative cocci and rods are added. After 1-4 days, fusobacteria and filaments appear. After 4-9 days, spirilla and spirochetes join the flora. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1600-0765.1966.tb01842.x
9. **Inflammation begins before you can see or feel it.** Sub-clinical inflammation started much earlier than the clinical diagnosis, probably as a reaction to the first phases of plaque development. The original study also saw a great increase in leukocytes (white blood cells) within days. https://www.perioexpertise.co.uk/summary-of-article-experimental-gingivitis-in-man/

**Weeks (the classic Löe experiment)**
10. **Gum inflammation in 10-21 days.** The 1965 experiment by Löe, Theilade and Jensen in J Periodontology is the landmark study. It showed that plaque formation is the only cause of reversible inflammatory changes of the gingivae. Skipping oral hygiene caused a dramatic increase in plaque and gum inflammation in all subjects, in all areas of the mouth, within 2-3 weeks. Studies show gingivitis develops within 10-21 days if all oral hygiene stops. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2147/PPA.S564132
11. **Reversible, with a catch.** After 21 days without brushing, the gingival index decreased markedly once oral hygiene restarted (study in people aged 70+). Neutrophils, the immune cells in the blood, can stay changed longer: the data suggest a lasting biological impact on neutrophil function even after clinical symptoms have abated. Neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and reactive oxygen species production were significantly elevated at day 21. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9141451/
12. **The inflammation reaches the bloodstream (PLoS ONE 2013).** In healthy young volunteers, gum inflammation was associated with a systemic rise in hsCRP (0.24 mg/L), IL-6 (12.52 ng/L) and MCP-1 between baseline and day 21, falling by day 42. Gum bleeding rose 34.0%. Caveat: it was a single-subject-style study, and a later 39-person study found elevated hs-CRP at day 21 that did not reach statistical significance. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23408963/ and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10420680/

**Months to years**
13. **Bacteria can enter the blood through damaged gums.** In extreme gingivitis or periodontitis, P. gingivalis can enter the bloodstream through inflamed and ulcerated periodontal tissue. A recent study found periodontitis patients have a 30% higher risk of atrial fibrillation. DNA from harmful oral bacteria has been found in heart muscle and valves. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7684789/ and https://www.asianscientist.com/2025/06/health/oral-bacteria-linked-to-heart-rhythm-disorder/
14. **Heart link: strong association, causation unproven.** The American Heart Association has noted that data are inconclusive on whether the periodontitis-CVD link is causal or coincidental. In mice, P. gingivalis infection caused bone resorption, aortic inflammation, viable bacteria in the aorta, and plaque development. Say "linked to", not "causes", in the video. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9893678/ and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4024021/
15. **The Alzheimer's connection (Science Advances 2019, Dominy et al.).** P. gingivalis was identified in the brains of Alzheimer's patients, and its toxic gingipain proteases correlated with tau and ubiquitin pathology. In mice, infection led to brain colonization and more Aβ1-42, a component of amyloid plaques. Caveat: a later review says these findings do not conclusively demonstrate a viable, actively colonizing infection. It is a hypothesis, not proven cause and effect. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau3333 and https://www.mdpi.com/2075-1729/15/1/96
16. **Gum disease can end in tooth loss.** If plaque buildup continues, it can permanently damage the gums and break down the bone supporting a tooth. WHO says the main risk factors for periodontal disease are poor oral hygiene and tobacco use. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/oral-health

**Scale and everyday hooks**
17. **WHO numbers.** Oral diseases affect nearly 3.7 billion people, and untreated tooth decay in permanent teeth is the most common health condition (GBD 2021). Severe periodontal disease affects more than 1 billion cases. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/oral-health
18. **Diabetes works both ways.** WHO notes diabetes has been linked in a reciprocal way with the development and progression of periodontal disease. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/oral-health
19. **Cost.** Direct treatment costs for severe periodontitis, caries and severe tooth loss are estimated at $357 billion, almost 5% of global healthcare spend. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41407-025-3105-9

## Myths and relatable moments
- **"Mouthwash replaces brushing."** One clinic source says mouth rinses and water flossers are relatively ineffective at removing dental plaque. Treat this as a clinic claim, not a trial result. https://www.eoperio.com.au/blog/dental-plaque
- **"Brushing alone is enough."** The same source says brushing alone reaches only about 60% of the tooth area. This is a weakly sourced figure. https://www.eoperio.com.au/blog/dental-plaque
- **The fuzzy-teeth feeling.** That fuzzy feel a few hours after brushing is early plaque. https://dentistexam.com/plaque-on-teeth/
- **Sipping and grazing is worse than a single treat.** If sugar is consumed repeatedly through the day, the mouth spends more time below the critical pH, raising decay risk. https://dentrends.com/denthink/how-sugar-affects-teeth-scientifically/
- **Brushing can cause brief bacteria in the blood.** Studies show P. gingivalis frequently enters the bloodstream through bleeding with tooth brushing or dental procedures, causing a transient bacteremia. This is a possible myth-buster for "bleeding gums mean stop brushing." Bleeding gums are a sign of inflammation, and the Löe studies show regular cleaning resolves it. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5078774/

## Gaps and cautions
- I could not retrieve CDC, NHS or Mayo Clinic pages in these searches. WHO, PubMed/PMC and peer-reviewed journals are the strongest sources above.
- The hour-level timings (items 3-7) rely on dental blogs and patent text. Check them against a textbook or review such as Marsh's work on oral biofilms before stating them as hard fact.
- "Never brushing for years" has no controlled trial for ethical reasons. The longest controlled data is the 21-day model, so anything beyond that is extrapolation from periodontitis research.
