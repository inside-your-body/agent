## SECONDS TO MINUTES

1. **A can is a full day's sugar allowance (WHO).** A single can of sugar-sweetened soda contains up to 40 grams (around 10 teaspoons) of free sugars. WHO's stricter 5% target is around 25 grams (about 6 teaspoons) a day for an average adult. One can therefore overshoots the ideal daily limit by about 60%. [Strong]
   https://who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children
   https://media.un.org/unifeed/en/asset/u140/u140305e

2. **Mouth bacteria react within seconds.** Within seconds of a sugary snack or drink, bacteria in plaque begin producing acid. Within two to three minutes, enough lactic acid builds up to drop plaque pH from about 7.0 to near 5.0. [Moderate: dental education sources, classic "Stephan curve" research]
   https://www.dentagum.co/blogs/news/stephan-curve-explained
   https://aboveandbeyond.dentist/article-sugar-attacks-enamel.dc

3. **Enamel dissolves below pH 5.5.** Hydrogen ions from bacterial acid displace calcium and phosphate from the enamel crystal lattice. In classic experiments plaque pH plunges below the threshold within 5–10 minutes, stays acidic for about 20 minutes, and takes 30–60 minutes to recover. [Moderate]
   https://www.innovasmilesdental.com/blog/how-sugar-damages-teeth
   https://www.lumidental.au/lumi-blog/sugar-and-tooth-decay-how-it-works-sydney

4. **Every sip restarts the clock.** This is the myth-buster for slow sippers. Damage depends less on how much acid you consume than on how often your mouth is pushed below the threshold, because each exposure resets the recovery clock. Sipping one can over 2 hours is worse for teeth than drinking it in 5 minutes. [Moderate]
   https://toothcheck.io/blog/mouth-ph-and-oral-health

## 10–60 MINUTES

5. **Blood sugar climbs and the pancreas responds.** Within the first 10 to 15 minutes, the intestines move the sugar into the blood, spiking blood sugar. The pancreas releases insulin to help transport the sugar to the muscles. This is a general-press source (Reader's Digest/The Healthy, quoting doctors), so it is better backed by a textbook or NIH page before filming. [Moderate]
   https://www.thehealthy.com/nutrition/what-happens-after-drinking-soda/

6. **The liver takes the fructose.** Fructose is metabolized rapidly, mainly in first-pass metabolism in the liver via fructokinase. This increases de novo lipogenesis (making new fat), triglycerides and uric acid. The same source notes it causes mitochondrial oxidant stress and a transient depletion of intracellular phosphate and ATP. [Strong: Journal of Hepatology]
   https://www.journal-of-hepatology.eu/article/S0168-8278(15)00348-7/fulltext

7. **Fructose uses the liver's fuel to make uric acid.** Fructose phosphorylation in the liver consumes ATP, and the accumulated ADP serves as the substrate for uric acid formation. Fructose is also the only sugar known to increase serum uric acid, which is linked to gout. [Strong]
   https://hbsn.amegroups.org/article/view/5213/html
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4592517/

8. **Blood pressure can rise within the sitting.** Clinically relevant doses (60 g) of fructose acutely raise blood pressure in humans, and similar increases followed ingestion of 24 ounces of HFCS- or sucrose-containing beverages. The authors suggest uric acid is likely involved. Note that this is a review article, and the 24 oz figure is a large drink. [Moderate]
   https://diabetesjournals.org/diabetes/article/62/10/3307/17686/Sugar-Uric-Acid-and-the-Etiology-of-Diabetes-and

## HOURS (APPETITE)

9. **Liquid sugar does not make you feel full.** The prevailing mechanisms linking sugary drinks to weight gain are decreased satiety and incomplete compensatory reduction in energy intake at subsequent meals. A 12-oz serving averages 140–150 calories and 35–37.5 g sugar. [Strong: AHA Circulation, Hu & Malik]
   https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circulationaha.109.876185

10. **Real-life numbers.** In one cohort, women who went from one sugary drink a week to one or more a day consumed an average of 358 extra calories per day and gained a significant amount of weight. In a meal study, with caloric beverages of 150 calories each, subjects consumed 105 more calories overall at each meal. Those two figures come from a Washington Post article, so find the primary papers before filming. [Moderate]
    https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/articles/A30903-2004Dec28.html

11. **Caveat (honest nuance):** the short-term "poor compensation" findings come from acute studies. Results from these acute studies should not be extrapolated to infer that liquid energy sources lead to weight gain over the long term. A small body of work on fructose and leptin or ghrelin is also still a hypothesis. [Strong: clinicaltrials.gov protocol language and AJCN review]
    https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02702440
    https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(22)10529-0/fulltext

## DAYS TO WEEKS

12. **Two weeks is enough to change blood fats, liver fat and insulin sensitivity.** In a UC Davis/NHLBI trial, sucrose- and HFCS-sweetened drinks induced detrimental changes in liver fat, insulin sensitivity, and circulating lipids, lipoproteins and uric acid in 2 weeks. Participants drank beverages with HFCS equal to 0, 10, 17.5 or 25 percent of daily calorie needs. It was the first study to show a direct dose-dependent relationship between added sugar in beverages and increases in specific cardiovascular risk factors. [Strong: PubMed]
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34265055/
    https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150422142515.htm

13. **The fructose-only effect.** Drinks sweetened with fructose and sucrose, but not glucose, increased the liver's ability to produce lipids. Even a few weeks of drinks with moderate fructose changes the serum fatty acid profile and induces hepatic insulin resistance. Over weeks, 24-h triglycerides rose much more at night with fructose than with glucose drinks. [Strong: Journal of Hepatology RCT; AJCN-linked review]
    https://www.journal-of-hepatology.eu/article/S0168-8278(21)00161-6/fulltext
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3649102/

14. **Metabolic syndrome in 2 weeks (extreme dose).** In a randomized trial of 74 men given 200 g fructose daily for 2 weeks, fasting triglycerides rose by about 55 mg/dl and HDL fell by about 2.5 mg/dl. 25% of the subjects developed de novo metabolic syndrome at 2 weeks. Allopurinol, which lowers uric acid, prevented the rise in 24-h ambulatory diastolic blood pressure. 200 g is far more than a normal drinker consumes, so say "extreme dose" on screen. [Strong, with the dose caveat]
    https://www.nature.com/articles/ijo2009259
    https://diabetesjournals.org/diabetes/article/62/10/3307/17686/Sugar-Uric-Acid-and-the-Etiology-of-Diabetes-and

## MONTHS TO YEARS (HABITUAL DRINKING)

15. **Fatty liver link.** Patients with biopsy-proven NAFLD consumed 2–3 fold more sugary drinks than matched controls. Caution: whether sugars at typical population levels affect liver fat independently of excess calories remains unresolved. The Framingham study also found decreasing sugary-drink intake was not associated with decreased liver fat. [Strong, with debate]
    https://www.journal-of-hepatology.eu/article/S0168-8278(15)00348-7/fulltext
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25514388/
    https://www.cghjournal.org/article/S1542-3565(21)01178-2/fulltext

16. **Type 2 diabetes risk.** One extra serving per day was associated with an 18% greater incidence of type 2 diabetes before adjustment for adiposity, and 13% after adjustment. The BMJ meta-analysis behind that figure covered 38,253 diabetes cases. A 2026 review puts it at a 13–30% rise per daily serving and says the form in which sugar is consumed appears more relevant than the type of sugar. [Strong: BMJ and PMC]
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4510779/
    https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12904972/

17. **Teeth, WHO's strongest evidence.** WHO says there are higher rates of dental caries when free sugars exceed 10% of energy. A WHO page calls tooth decay the most prevalent noncommunicable disease globally. Children with the highest intake of sugary drinks are more likely to be overweight or obese. [Strong: WHO]
    https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/free-sugars-adults-ncds
    https://diabetesonthenet.com/diabetes-news/who-guidelines-call-to-slash-sugar-intake-in-adults-and-children/

## MYTHS TO BUST

18. **"Phosphoric acid is the only thing stopping you from vomiting."** False. UC Davis's Kimber Stanhope said her team has studied hundreds of participants who consumed beverages with more than 10 teaspoons of sugar but no phosphoric acid. Juices also contain about 10 teaspoons without it. [Strong]
    https://www.buzzfeed.com/carolynkylstra/heres-whats-wrong-with-that-viral-coca-cola-graphic

19. **"Coke acts on your brain like heroin."** Overblown. Coca-Cola won't make you throw up and doesn't simulate heroin. A neuroscientist quoted said caffeine enhances the release of dopamine, not direct "production". The viral graphic's content was lifted from a 2010 Blisstree article and the same text has circulated since at least 2006. [Strong as a debunk]
    https://uproxx.com/viral/coca-cola-infographic/
    https://www.dailyedge.ie/coke-infographic-viral-2249164-Aug2015/

20. **"It's not all doom."** Consuming sugar-sweetened beverages in moderation is unlikely to have a severe effect on health. The risk is dose- and frequency-driven. [Moderate]
    https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/297600
