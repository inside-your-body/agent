## Timeline: minutes to hours

- **10–60 min: caffeine peaks.** A typical cup (25–40 mg caffeine) peaks in plasma at about 1–3 μg/ml, 30–60 minutes after drinking. Tea's peak may come a little later than coffee's (45–90 min vs 30–60 min), but the total caffeine absorbed is about the same. ⚠️ https://mikeydoes.com/glossary/tea-caffeine-adenosine/
- **~48 min: L-theanine peaks in blood.** After 100 mg of L-theanine, plasma peaked at 48 minutes, from both capsules and green tea. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228460064_Human_disposition_of_L-theanine_in_tea_or_aqueous_solution
- **~30–40 min: L-theanine reaches the brain and alpha waves rise.** It crosses the blood-brain barrier through the LAT1 transporter, the same carrier that moves leucine and phenylalanine. A 2008 EEG study found that a single 50 mg dose significantly increased alpha-wave activity, the brainwave pattern of calm wakefulness. The effect is subtle and varies between people. https://aotea.store/blogs/tea-blog/what-is-l-theanine-tea
- **Hook: caffeine plus theanine.** Caffeine alone doesn't promote alpha waves, but EEG studies show the combination gives higher alpha patterns than either compound alone. ⚠️ https://mikeydoes.com/glossary/tea-caffeine-adenosine/
- **1–2 h: catechins (EGCG) peak, then vanish.** In a pharmacokinetic study, EGCG plasma peaks came 1.3–2.2 hours after a dose, with a half-life of 1.9–4.6 hours. EGCG is poorly absorbed, reaches the blood at very low micromolar levels, and disappears from plasma within several hours. https://www.mdpi.com/1422-0067/20/15/3630
- **Only a sliver gets in.** After tea ingestion over 6 hours, about 0.16% of the ingested catechins were found in plasma. The same paper gives urine at 1.1%. In mice, only 0.16% of digested tea polyphenols enter the bloodstream. The rest goes to the gut. https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)13786-7/pdf and https://advances.nutrition.org/article/S2161-8313(25)00183-8/pdf
- **~5 h: caffeine half-life.** The mean plasma half-life of caffeine is about 5 hours, but it can range from 1.5 to 9.5 hours. That is why a 4 pm tea can still be active at bedtime. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2024.2323919

## Hours: the gut

- **2–48 h: gut bacteria take over.** EGCG is absorbed in the intestine within hours (<8 h), and its metabolites are then absorbed from the large intestine (>8–48 h). In lab fermentation, the gut microbiota needed up to 12 hours to adapt to green tea catechins, and its composition had changed by 12 hours. The colon acts as a "bioreactor" with metabolic genes the human genome lacks. https://doi.org/10.3390/foods13050792 and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10930907/
- **Prebiotic effect (in vitro).** About 50 mg/L of EGCG or TFDG raised Bifidobacterium abundance 4-fold at 24 h and 10-fold at 48 h. This is a test-tube result, not a human trial. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S092422442200200X

## Days: blood pressure

- **~1 week: small blood pressure drop.** A meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (378 people) found that daily black tea for ≥1 week was associated with a significant drop in systolic and diastolic pressure of about 2 and 1 mmHg. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0103247

## Weeks to months

- **4 weeks: results are mixed.** One RCT in 100 mildly hypertensive type 2 diabetics found that three glasses of green or sour tea daily for 4 weeks significantly lowered systolic and diastolic pressure. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23725524/ In contrast, a four-week crossover trial in normotensive people found six mugs of tea daily had no significant effect on clinic blood pressure. https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/11/5/1115
- **A 2022 meta-analysis found no effect at 4–12 weeks.** None of the 3 included RCTs reported significant or clinically important changes in blood pressure over 4–12 weeks. The honest story is that the effect is small and slow. https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)02404-X/fulltext
- **~6 months: the strongest trial result.** In an Australian RCT, 95 regular tea drinkers had 3 cups of black tea a day for ≥6 months and saw systolic and diastolic drops of 2–3 mmHg. https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/11/5/1115
- **Tolerance builds.** Regular tea and coffee drinkers develop a profound tolerance to caffeine's diuretic and other effects. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2024.2323919

## Years: long-term risk

- **UK Biobank, about 500,000 people over a median of 11.2 years.** Higher tea intake was modestly associated with lower all-cause mortality among those drinking 2 or more cups a day. The hazard ratio was 0.87 for 2–3 cups a day versus none. The study is observational, so it shows association, not cause. https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M22-0041
- **Plateau effect.** A meta-analysis of 38 cohorts found that the biggest drop in all-cause mortality was at 2 cups a day, and more tea did not appear to lower risk further. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11573487/
- **Heart disease.** A Dutch cohort found 3–6 cups of tea a day was associated with reduced coronary heart disease mortality. https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/atvbaha.109.201939

## Surprising facts and myths

- **Surprise: tea blocks iron.** Tannins and catechins bind non-heme iron in the gut. Tea polyphenols act as inhibitors of non-haem iron uptake, which can potentially lead to poor iron status. Reported reductions are about 60–70% when tea is drunk with a meal. ⚠️ Waiting 1 hour before or after meals cuts the interference by about 80%. Heme iron from meat is largely unaffected. Estimates vary widely (20% to 87%) between sources, and one narrative review argues that saliva proteins may blunt the effect in vivo (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5525358/). Relatable angle: tea with a lentil, spinach or fortified-cereal meal. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05019573 and https://teatrade.co.uk/learning/tea-and-iron-absorption
- **Myth: tea dehydrates you.** A short-term diuretic response appears mainly above ~250–300 mg of caffeine taken acutely by caffeine-deprived people. A cup has about 25–70 mg. In the 2016 Beverage Hydration Index study, 72 men drank 1 L of hot or iced tea and produced no more urine over 4 hours than after water. The NHS says that tea and coffee count towards your fluid intake. https://getvari.app/faq/is-tea-a-diuretic and https://mikeydoes.com/glossary/tea-and-hydration/
- **The real danger is temperature, not tea.** IARC classified drinking very hot beverages (above 65 °C) as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A), especially for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma. A Golestan, Iran cohort found that drinking 700 mL/day or more of tea at 60 °C or above carried a 75% higher oesophageal cancer risk (vs. under 700 mL at under 60 °C), while tea below 60 °C showed no increased risk. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK606451/box/Bax/?report=objectonly
- **Newer UK data point to a lower threshold.** In 977,282 UK adults, preferring tea or coffee "very hot" was linked to more than three times the risk of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma compared with "warm". Typical UK tea is drunk at 54–62 °C, below IARC's threshold. Tip for the viewer: let it cool a few minutes. https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260914/Like-your-tea-or-coffee-very-hot-A-study-of-nearly-1-million-adults-found-a-striking-cancer-link.aspx and https://www.nature.com/articles/s41416-025-02953-2
- **Liver: brewed tea is fine, extract capsules are not.** EFSA considers catechins from brewed, instant and ready-to-drink green tea generally safe. For supplements, 800 mg/day of EGCG may be associated with initial signs of liver damage. A cup of brewed green tea has roughly 50–100 mg of EGCG (⚠️ secondary source). https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/180418
- **More isn't always better.** One MASLD cohort found that 7 or more cups a day was associated with a modestly higher cardiovascular risk than none, though the result was not significant after weighting. A Welsh cohort showed something similar for more than 8 cups a day. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1945413/full
