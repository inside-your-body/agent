Most of the cell-level timing below comes from mouse or rat studies, as marked. I didn't find WHO, CDC, NHS or Mayo pages on this topic, so the sources are PubMed/PMC, Nature, PNAS, Cell Reports and similar journals.

## Minutes (the chemistry)

1. **The first fix happens within 5–10 minutes.**
   - Rapid stabilizing events occur in the 5–10 min after learning, while much later processes, likely involving protein synthesis, maintain the memory trace.
   - Source: J Neurosci (integrin dynamics) https://www.jneurosci.org/content/32/37/12854

2. **A second wave arrives about 40–60 minutes later.**
   - In mice, blocking β1 integrin 20 min after learning prevented long-term object-location memory, but blocking it 40 min later did not.
   - Researchers also found a previously undetected phase of LTP consolidation 40–60 min after induction.
   - Source: same J Neurosci paper above.

3. **Short-term and long-term memory run on different machinery.**
   - Early LTP lasts 2–3 h and doesn't need protein synthesis. Late LTP lasts weeks in vivo and needs new proteins.
   - Source: Physiological Reviews, "Long-Term Potentiation and Memory" https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/physrev.00014.2003

4. **Blocking protein synthesis erases the memory but not the moment.**
   - Protein synthesis inhibitors can block persistent memory but leave short-term memory unaffected.
   - That is why you can "get" something and still forget it by tomorrow.
   - Source: same Physiological Reviews article.

## Hours (new genes, new proteins)

5. **The key protein window is about 1–3 hours after learning.**
   - De novo protein synthesis occurs during certain time windows (∼1–3 h after learning).
   - Source: Learning & Memory https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/26/9/299.full

6. **Recalling a memory makes it fragile again.**
   - Memory retrieval can induce a transient instability during which the memory is again susceptible to protein synthesis inhibition.
   - That is followed by a period of restabilization.
   - Every time you remember something, you partly rebuild it.
   - Source: arXiv model paper summarising the reconsolidation literature https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.00683

7. **Sleep is when new synapses get built.**
   - In mice, motor training progressively increased new spine formation over 6 to 48 hours.
   - Source: Science (Yang et al.), via PMC https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4447313/
   - Sleep after learning also promotes dendritic filopodia and spines clustered with learning-inactive existing spines.
   - Source: PNAS https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2114856118

8. **Sleep both strengthens and prunes.**
   - During waking, synaptic strength is enhanced (upscaling), and sleep counterbalances this by weakening synaptic connections (downscaling).
   - Another review describes global downscaling that optimises network efficiency alongside local plasticity that selectively strengthens memory-relevant synapses.
   - Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6694322/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13674668

9. **While you sleep, the hippocampus replays the day to the cortex.**
   - Ripples are thought to represent memory trace events replayed during sleep, transferring memory representations to cortex for long-term storage.
   - In humans, sleep significantly increased the rate of glymphatic clearance compared to wake.
   - In animals, 7 consecutive days of sleep restriction impaired glymphatic tracer flow and cognition.
   - Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9616228/

## Days

10. **Synapses turn over fast, even in a resting brain.**
    - In mouse hippocampus, researchers measured a spine turnover of ~40% within 4 days.
    - Source: eLife https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6014725/

11. **A peak of new synapses appears 8–24 hours after practice.**
    - New spine formation increases in the mouse motor cortex 8–24 h after motor training.
    - Source: Cell Reports https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211124722010464

12. **Your brain's "before" state predicts how well you'll learn.**
    - Spine turnover before learning predicts future learning and memory performance, as well as the localization and rates of spine clustering.
    - Source: Nature Communications https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-017-02751-2

## Weeks

13. **Six weeks of juggling changed white matter in adults.**
    - Volunteers got weekly juggling lessons for six weeks and were asked to practice at least half an hour each day.
    - MRI then showed a localized increase in fractional anisotropy, a measure of microstructure, in white matter underlying the intraparietal sulcus.
    - Surprise: all participants showed white-matter changes, suggesting this reflected time spent practising rather than skill attained.
    - Sources: Wiley Analytical Science https://analyticalscience.wiley.com/content/article-do/juggling-matters-brain and Oxford/ScienceDaily https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/10/091016114055.htm

14. **Slower learners showed more myelin change.**
    - In a 4-week, 10-session training study, a slower rate of learning was associated with greater change in myelin water fraction in the left IPS.
    - Source: PMC https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4884808/
    - Caveat: MRI is an indirect way to measure brain structure, so we cannot be sure exactly what is changing.

15. **White matter and grey matter change on different clocks.**
    - After the jugglers stopped training, white-matter changes seemed activity-related, while grey-matter changes continued even after 4 weeks without training, suggesting a more sluggish mechanism.
    - Source: Scientific American https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/watching-the-brain-learn/

## Months and years

16. **New habits take a median of about 66 days, but the range is huge.**
    - UCL's study recruited 96 people who wanted a new habit, such as eating fruit with lunch or a 15-minute daily run.
    - Habits took anywhere from 18 to 254 days to form.
    - Fine print: the 66 days is the median time to reach 95% of a modeled automaticity plateau for 39 participants whose data met the researchers' criteria.
    - Missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation.
    - Source: Lally et al., Eur J Soc Psychol 2010;40(6):998–1009 https://www.spring.org.uk/2024/11/form-habit-66.php (secondary summary) and https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit

17. **London taxi trainees physically grew part of their hippocampus.**
    - Trainees spend 3–4 years learning the city's layout.
    - Those who qualified showed a selective increase in gray matter volume in their posterior hippocampi.
    - Trainees who failed had no such change, though they were exposed to the same milieu.
    - Earlier work found taxi drivers had greater gray matter volume in mid-posterior hippocampi and less in anterior hippocampi than bus drivers.
    - Sources: Current Biology via PMC https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3268356/ and Maguire 2006 on PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17024677/

## Rewiring after injury (the extreme case)

- **After a stroke, repair starts at about 1–4 weeks.**
  - Surviving neurons initiate axonal outgrowth and synaptogenesis, particularly in the peri-infarct regions.
  - Source: PMC review https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13447379/
- **The "3–6 month window" is not a hard deadline.**
  - It is widely accepted, but an analysis of 219 individuals with mild to moderate upper-limb hemiparesis found enhanced sensitivity to treatment that expands far beyond the limits of the so-called critical window.
  - Source: J Neurophysiol, PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31141442/

## Myths to bust

- **"21 days to form a habit."**
  - This is a misreading of Maxwell Maltz's 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics, which was about plastic-surgery patients' psychological adjustment.
  - Source: https://taskcoach.ai/blog/habit-formation-real-curve-lally-66-days/ (secondary source)
- **"Adults never grow new neurons."**
  - This is still debated, not settled. A 2018 Nature paper concluded neurogenesis in the hippocampus is not seen beyond childhood in humans.
  - A 2019 Nature Medicine study of post-mortem tissue from healthy adults aged 43–87 reported newborn neurons and a modest decline with age.
  - A recent overview says frequency, distribution and functional contribution remain active research questions.
  - Sources: Nature editorial https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-019-00891-7 and https://www.psykholoh.com/en/post/adult-neurogenesis-what-research-can-and-cannot-establish
- **"You only use 10% of your brain" or "the adult brain is fixed."**
  - I didn't search these directly, so I have no source to cite. The studies above (white-matter change in adults, hippocampal growth, post-stroke recovery) contradict the second one.

## Everyday hooks for the video

- Learning something at 6pm and sleeping on it is a measurable biological step, not a cliché.
- A "slow" learner may be making a bigger structural change (fact 14).
- Missing one gym or habit day doesn't reset the process.
- Re-reading or recalling a memory makes it briefly editable (fact 6).

## Caveats

- Most minute-to-day timings (facts 1–2, 5, 7, 10–12) come from rodent studies, not humans.
- The human data (juggling, taxi, habits) are MRI or behavioural, so they show that change happens, not exactly which cells did it.
- The habit-timing and stroke "golden window" claims rest partly on secondary sources, so check the primary papers before quoting exact numbers on screen.
