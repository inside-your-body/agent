## The core mechanism (organs, cells, molecules)

1. **Normal sodium range.** A healthy blood sodium level is 135–145 mmol/L, and hyponatremia begins when it falls below 135. Sodium is the "salt" that keeps water balanced inside and outside cells. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hyponatremia/symptoms-causes/syc-20373711)

2. **The kidney has a speed limit.** The kidneys can handle a maximal excretion rate of 0.7 to 1.0 L per hour. The danger depends on rate, not daily total. 4 liters across a day is unremarkable while 4 liters in four hours is not. [Source on kidney limits](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11624634_Peak_rates_of_diuresis_in_healthy_humans_during_oral_fluid_overload) · [Rate vs. daily total](https://healthcalculator.io/guides/how-much-water-is-too-much/)

3. **What happens at the cell level.** Excess water migrates by diffusion toward sites with a higher solute concentration. Intracellular water rises and cells swell. [StatPearls: Water Toxicity (NIH/NCBI)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK537231/)

4. **The skull is a closed box.** When brain cells swell inside a fixed skull, pressure builds rapidly. Swollen neurons raise intracranial pressure, causing confusion, lethargy, headache and drowsiness, and can cause bradycardia and hypertension. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK537231/)

5. **A vicious loop (surprising).** Nausea can trigger release of anti-diuretic hormone (ADH), which causes water retention. This feedback loop can worsen hyponatremia. This comes from a patent background section, so treat it as secondary and verify it before using it on screen. [Source](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8518895)

## Timeline: minutes → hours → days → weeks

6. **Minutes to the first hour.** Mild symptoms come first. Mild symptoms are nausea, vomiting and bloating. Severe symptoms may include confusion, seizure and coma. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/water-intoxication)

7. **Hours, the "acute" window.** Severe symptomatic hyponatremia occurs when sodium falls over less than 24 hours. Severe symptoms such as coma and seizures typically occur below 120 mEq/L, but can occur below 125. [AAFP 2015](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2015/0301/p299.html)

8. **Mortality of the acute form.** Hyponatremic encephalopathy, the consequence of brain swelling from acute hyponatremia, is associated with an overall mortality of 34%. The figure comes from a 2014 review. [MDPI J Clin Med](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/3/4/1163)

9. **The brain fights back within hours.** Within a few hours cells lose electrolytes (rapid adaptation), and later organic osmolytes (slow adaptation). This reduces swelling and normalizes brain volume. In chronic hyponatremia (>48 h), brain cells lose electrolytes and organic osmolytes such as myoinositol, betaine, glutamine, taurine and GABA. [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/3/4/1163) · [Frontiers in Medicine](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2021.693738/full)

10. **Slow = quiet, fast = dangerous (counterintuitive).** Slowly developing hyponatremia, even to very severe degrees, is not complicated by significant brain swelling. Roughly half of patients with chronic hyponatremia are asymptomatic even with sodium below 125 mEq/L. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2021.693738/full) · [PMC seizure review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4712283/)

11. **Days: the kidney adapts, but the safety margin shrinks.** Chronic overhydration for more than 3 days increases urinary free-water excretion by raising aquaporin-2 water-channel expression in the renal collecting duct. It also raises renal solute loss, which lowers the threshold for water intoxication. [Source](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11624634_Peak_rates_of_diuresis_in_healthy_humans_during_oral_fluid_overload)

12. **Weeks to months: mostly a disease of chronic polydipsia, not healthy people.** One patient with schizoaffective disorder was found drinking over 15 liters daily, including from storage tanks and washing areas. This is a case report, not a general rule. In psychogenic polydipsia, symptoms rarely occur unless patients keep drinking over 10 litres/day after maximum urine dilution is reached. [PubMed case](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41376739/) · [Case Reports in Psychiatry](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2015/846459)

13. **The twist: treatment can damage the brain too.** Overly rapid correction causes an inverse osmotic gradient, brain dehydration and demyelination of white matter. ODS is more likely when sodium is below 105 mEq/L, and malnutrition, alcohol use and low potassium add risk. ODS has also occurred with sodium below 115 mEq/L even when correction was ≤10 mEq/L per 24 h. [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/3/4/1163) · [AAFP 2023](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2023/1100/sodium-disorders-hyponatremia-hypernatremia.html) · [ODS despite guidelines](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353114142_Osmotic_Demyelination_Syndrome_following_Correction_of_Hyponatremia_by_10_mEqL_per_Day)

## Severity thresholds (useful for on-screen graphics)

14. Hyponatremia is mild at 130–134 mEq/L, moderate at 125–129 and severe below 125. Mild symptoms are nausea, vomiting, weakness and headache. Severe symptoms include delirium, seizures and, rarely, brain herniation and death. Classification cut-offs vary by guideline. [AAFP 2023](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2023/1100/sodium-disorders-hyponatremia-hypernatremia.html)

## Real-world cases and everyday situations

15. **Radio contest (2007).** Jennifer Strange drank roughly two gallons over about three hours. She said she looked pregnant because her belly was swollen with water, and complained that her head hurt. She went home, collapsed, and was found dead about six hours later. The coroner ruled the cause water intoxication, or hyponatremia. Reported volumes vary between outlets (roughly 1.5 gallons to 6–7 L). [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna16815105) · [CourtDocket](https://courtdocket.org/what-happened-in-the-hold-your-wee-for-a-wii-lawsuit/) · [Insider Exclusive](https://insiderexclusive.com/radios-deadly-stunts-the-jennifer-strange-story/)

16. **Marathons and endurance sport (the relatable scenario).** Because you lose sodium through sweat, drinking too much water during marathons and triathlons can dilute blood sodium. The prevalence of exercise-associated hyponatremia in marathon runners ranges from under 1 to 22%. Excessive fluid consumption and prolonged finishing times were the primary risk factors, and vomiting was the only clinical sign distinguishing it from other causes of collapse. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hyponatremia/symptoms-causes/syc-20373711) · [MDPI J Clin Med 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/11/22/6775)

17. **Drinking by thirst works.** The Third International Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia Consensus concluded that drinking according to thirst is sufficient to prevent both dehydration and hyponatremia. [Wikipedia summary of the 2015 consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exercise-associated_hyponatremia) (the primary statement is in Clin J Sport Med, 2015).

18. **Military and heat.** One military review led to a recommendation of no more than 1–1.5 L of water per hour of heavy sweating, after three soldiers died. Even people resting quietly in extreme heat or humidity may be at risk if they drink large amounts over short periods. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_intoxication) (secondary; check the cited primary papers before use).

## Myths and surprises

19. **Myth: "flush toxins with 3–4 L fast."** Drinking 3–4 liters quickly to "flush out toxins" is unsupported by evidence and is called the most common pathway to inadvertent hyponatremia in healthy young adults. This is a lower-quality app-blog source, so don't use the claim as a statistic without a better one. [Source](https://water-tracker.app/en/blog/hyponatremia-overhydration-risks)

20. **Myth: "healthy people are in constant danger."** With working kidneys, drinking too much water almost never drops sodium too low unless you're very dehydrated (like after a marathon) and don't replace electrolytes. A 2023 *eBioMedicine* letter notes that concerns about fatal water intoxication are often overestimated, since even minimally diseased kidneys can excrete up to 24 L per day. That daily figure assumes a steady rate, so it conflicts with the per-hour caution above. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/17762-hyponatremia) · [eBioMedicine, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10227416/)

21. **Surprise: beer can do it.** Beer contains little electrolytes or protein, so it gives too little solute to drive water excretion, diluting the blood and causing severe hyponatremia. To excrete water, the kidney must also excrete solute. This is a Wikipedia source, so verify it against the primary literature. [Potomania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potomania)

22. **Surprise: a "normal" low-salt diet raises risk.** In the Olympus Marathon study, dietary sodium intake was lower in hyponatremic runners than in runners with normal sodium. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7230381/)

## Suggested video structure (timeline hook)

- **0–60 min:** gut absorbs, kidneys hit the ~1 L/h ceiling, blood sodium dilutes.
- **1–4 h:** cells swell, headache and nausea, the nausea-ADH loop, confusion.
- **4–24 h (acute only):** seizures, coma, brain herniation, respiratory arrest.
- **Days:** the brain dumps osmolytes to adapt, and symptoms can go quiet.
- **Weeks and months:** polydipsia, low sodium, and the risk of overly fast correction (ODS).
