## Baseline: what a blink does

1. **Normal rate.** Spontaneous blinks occur on average 14 to 22 times per minute. That is a blink every 3 to 4 seconds. Source: Scientific Reports. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-94568-9
2. **Each blink is a mini-blackout.** Each blink lasts 100–150 ms, obstructs all pattern vision and attenuates light levels 100-fold. Source: Current Biology. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982205007165
3. **The brain hides it.** Blinking suppressed brain activity in the visual cortex and in parietal and prefrontal areas that are usually activated when people become conscious of visual events. That is why you never notice the darkness. At about 15 blinks a minute, we spend at least 9 days per year blinking. Source: UCL/Current Biology via Phys.org. https://phys.org/news/2005-07-brain.amp
4. **Each blink is a wiper, not just a lubricant.** The eyelid wipes over the cornea, smoothing the mucin layer and spreading the aqueous and lipid layers to give a completely wetted surface. The lipid layer forms on the upstroke of each blink, as meibomian lipid spreads upward over the aqueous layer. Sources: https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11229549 and https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/tear-film-lipid
5. **The tear film is tiny.** The tear film (7.9 μl) is regenerated after each blink, and it is completely replenished every 5 to 6 minutes. Source (a supplier's educational page, so treat it as secondary): https://trbchemedica.co.uk/blogs/understanding-the-tear-film/the-distribution-system

## The no-blink timeline

6. **0–10 seconds: the film starts thinning.** Between blinks, the tear film thins as the aqueous layer evaporates. Many people complain of eye discomfort if the time between blinks exceeds 10-12 seconds. Source: patent text citing clinical data, so use cautiously. https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10952606
7. **~10–30 seconds: first dry spots.** In a healthy eye, the tear film begins to evaporate after 15 to 30 seconds between blinks. In the clinic, when blinking is prevented, the tear film becomes progressively thinner and eventually breaks up, showing breakup spots over the cornea. A tear breakup time of ≥10 s is considered normal. Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S089289670000033X and https://iovs.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2350415
8. **Molecular mechanism of the breakup.** Between blinks, lipid molecules migrate through the aqueous layer toward the mucin layer. This contaminates the mucin, raises surface tension, and makes the film rupture and bead up into a dry spot. A blink normally repairs this. Source: patent literature. https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11679078
9. **Salt spike and the sting.** As water evaporates, tears get saltier. Tear osmolarity is approximately 312 mOsm/L under physiological conditions. A study of corneal nerves estimated a spontaneous blink could be triggered at a tear osmolarity of approximately 400 mOsm/L. Spikes in saltiness may transiently occur during tear instability, causing corneal inflammation and triggering sensory neurons. Sources: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12649220/ and https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5256685
10. **Minutes: the cornea's nerves get dulled.** The cornea contains "dry-sensitive" neurons that trigger tearing. In experiments, their dry responses were depressed or even abolished by hyperosmolar tears, depending on time (30 minutes to 3 hours) and dose (450–1000 mOsm). The surprising part is that the alarm system itself gets damaged. Source: IOVS/PubMed. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26720465/
11. **Cell stress inside the cornea.** In lab studies, hyperosmotic stress induced corneal epithelial cell senescence through mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, DNA damage and compromised autophagy. The TFOS DEWS II report calls evaporation-induced tear hyperosmolarity the core mechanism of dry eye, producing a vicious circle. Sources: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12649220/ and https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/70037
12. **30 min to 4 hours: measured in mice.** Researchers used eye speculums to hold mouse eyelids open for 30 min, 1 h, 2 h or 4 h. Air exposure triggered autophagy, which is indispensable for corneal epithelial cell survival. The study also showed apoptosis and inflammation increased when autophagy was blocked. Source: Journal of Cellular and Molecular Medicine. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6533494/
13. **Hours: the cornea starts to die.** In exposed cornea, any exposed epithelium quickly becomes desiccated, causing cell membrane damage and death. The result is a painful red eye that looks like a corneal abrasion under cobalt-blue light. Source: ophthalmology reference (ScienceDirect topics). https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/exposure-keratopathy
14. **Hours to days: blindness risk.** Exposure keratopathy can result in destruction of the cornea and blindness if not treated urgently. If severe, there is a very high risk of irreversible blindness within a matter of hours or days. Source: Community Eye Health Journal (PMC). https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6253321/
15. **Days to weeks: infection and melting.** ICU patients with incomplete lid closure show the real-world sequence. Exposure keratopathy is the most common ocular surface complication in ICU patients and can bring bacterial ulcers, corneal perforation or opacity, and profound vision loss. One case developed purulent discharge with corneal infiltration within a few days. Prolonged cases can lead to progressive corneal thinning and even perforation. Sources: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7535751/ and the ScienceDirect link above.
16. **Months: scarring and permanent loss.** The main complication is permanent vision loss due to corneal opacification. The cornea is also one of the most densely innervated tissues in the body, so the pain would be intense until the nerves are damaged. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposure_keratopathy (secondary) and https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9872901

## Surprising angles and myths

17. **Your cornea breathes air.** As an avascular surface, the primary source of oxygen for the cornea comes from the atmosphere. The open eye gets oxygen via tears. With the eye closed, oxygen comes mainly from the capillaries of the upper eyelid. So staying open means no eyelid-blood oxygen backup, and sleeping with eyes closed is not "oxygen deprivation." Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/immunology-and-microbiology/tear-film and https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9618773
18. **Myth: tears alone protect you.** Exposure keratopathy occurs despite normal tear production when lids don't close. Tears need the blink to be spread. Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/exposure-keratopathy
19. **Myth: you can't stop blinking anyway.** You can hold off for a while, but discomfort rises sharply. Studies found the maximum blink interval is inversely associated with the rate of increasing discomfort, with a sharp increase in discomfort at tear breakup. The reflex wins. Source: Scientific Reports (same link as #1).
20. **Everyday situation: screens.** Office workers blink 22 times per minute relaxed, 10 per minute reading a book and 7 per minute viewing text on a video screen. In children, blink rate fell to 8.9 blinks/min from a baseline of 20.8 after as little as one minute of gaming. Blinks also become incomplete, which worsens dryness. Sources: https://www.reviewofoptometry.com/article/blinking-dynamics-contribute-to-ded and https://www.nature.com/articles/s41433-022-02122-2
21. **Everyday situation: sleep and illness.** Nocturnal lagophthalmos (being unable to close the eyelids during sleep) may also cause exposure keratopathy. Reduced muscle tone in Parkinson's can decrease blink rate and cause exposure. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposure_keratopathy and https://www.nweyes.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/April-2015-OD-Newsletter.pdf
22. **Bell's phenomenon is only a partial backup.** The eyes roll upward when the lids close, but it is absent in 25% of patients and does not reliably protect the ocular surface. Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/exposure-keratopathy
