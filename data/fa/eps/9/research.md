## Context: the numbers
1. **We eat about double the limit.** The global mean intake in 2021 was 4,278 mg/day sodium (about 11 g salt), more than double WHO's recommended less than 2,000 mg sodium (under 5 g salt, about a teaspoon). https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/sodium-reduction
2. **Hypertension share.** Excess sodium raises blood pressure and accounts for about 30% of hypertension prevalence. https://www.paho.org/en/topics/salt-reduction
3. **The "salt vs sodium" trap.** 1 g of salt equals 400 mg of sodium. The sodium figure on a food label is therefore only about 40% of the salt weight. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/sodium-reduction
4. **We barely need any.** Sodium deficiency is extremely unlikely in healthy people. The minimum physiological need is estimated at under 500 mg/day sodium. https://www.paho.org/en/enlace/salt-intake

## The timeline

**Seconds to minutes: sensing the salt**
5. **A brain sodium sensor.** The Na_X protein excites vasopressin-releasing neurons when blood sodium rises, as it does after dehydration or a significant sodium intake. This instructs the kidney to conserve water. https://healthenews.mcgill.ca/new-understanding-of-the-physiological-response-to-dehydration-and-salt-intake/
6. **Tiny changes trigger it.** Fluctuations in plasma osmolality as small as 1% induce vasopressin release. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7189486/

**30–60 minutes: arteries**
7. **The most surprising moment: blood vessels react in 30 minutes.** In a trial of 16 healthy people, a meal with a typical amount of salt significantly suppressed brachial artery flow-mediated dilation (FMD) within 30 min.
   - At 30 min, FMD was 3.39% after the high-salt meal versus 6.05% after the low-salt meal. At 60 min it was 2.20% versus 4.64%.
   - Blood pressure did not change significantly. The damage appears before any blood pressure rise.
   - The impairment was said to be of similar magnitude to that seen after a high-saturated-fat meal.
   - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916523022219 and https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2011/02/07/High-salt-intake-may-have-immediate-effects-on-circulation-Study/
8. **A likely molecular culprit is nitric oxide.** One proposed mechanism is suppression of nitric oxide formation. FMD measures endothelium-dependent, NO-mediated vasodilation. Lower FMD indicates impaired endothelial function and predicts adverse outcomes. https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jhypertension/fulltext/10.1097/hjh.0b013e32835e8de1~vascular-effects-of-high-salt-intake

**Hours: thirst, water hold-back, bloating**
9. **The thirst-and-hold-water double act.** After a salty meal, blood sodium rises and the body signals thirst and releases vasopressin. Vasopressin tells the kidneys to hold onto water, and thirst drives you to drink. https://scienceinsights.org/is-salt-a-diuretic-or-does-it-cause-water-retention/
10. **Puffy rings and bloating.** Some people notice bloating and puffiness within a few hours of a very high-sodium meal. One non-peer-reviewed source says the water retention typically lasts 24–48 hours. Treat that duration as approximate. https://wellkr.com/does-salt-help-you-retain-water-understanding-the-science-of-sodium and https://sciencewatch.blog/is-salt-a-diuretic-water-retention
11. **The body has a hidden salt store.** Skin, muscle and possibly vessel walls can hold sodium without extra water.
    - Sodium binds to negatively charged glycosaminoglycans, so it can be stored, particularly in skin, without water retention.
    - The buffering capacity in healthy people was estimated at about 50 mmol (about 3 g salt) after an acute hypertonic infusion.
    - Skin sodium has been reported as high as 180–190 mmol/L with no matching rise in water.
    - Caveat: some rodent studies have contested the glycosaminoglycan-binding idea. That sodium can accumulate in skin and muscle is now generally accepted, but how it influences blood pressure is not.
    - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7816310/, https://www.enbpr.org/search.php?where=aview&id=10.5049%2FEBP.2022.20.1.1&code=0158EBP&vmode=FULL and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10863658

**Days to weeks: blood pressure and immune cells**
12. **One week is enough to see it.** In an American Heart Association-reported randomized trial, a low-sodium diet significantly lowered blood pressure in 70–75% of participants in as little as one week, including people already on blood pressure medication. The flip side is that a salty diet keeps blood pressure higher. https://newsroom.heart.org/news/reducing-sodium-intake-significantly-lowered-blood-pressure-in-as-little-as-one-week
13. **Weeks to months: it keeps improving.** In DASH-Sodium, weekly BP change on the low-sodium diet was -0.94/-0.70 mm Hg per week, with no change on the high-sodium diet. The full effect of sodium reduction is not completely achieved by 4 weeks. https://pure.johnshopkins.edu/en/publications/time-course-of-change-in-blood-pressure-from-sodium-reduction-and/
14. **Salt reaches your immune system.** In the 2013 Nature studies, adding 40 mM NaCl to culture medium, similar to interstitial levels in animals on a high-salt diet, enhanced and stabilised production of pathogenic TH17 cytokines.
    - A high-salt diet also accelerated multiple sclerosis-like disease in mice.
    - Later work found high salt changed the gut microbiome, reduced intestinal survival of Lactobacillus, increased TH17 cells and raised blood pressure in humans.
    - Caveat: this is largely mouse and cell work. One small human study found an abrupt switch to a high-salt diet decreased Th17 cells. The human picture is mixed.
    - https://www.nature.com/articles/nri3432, https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24628 and https://www.nature.com/articles/srep26767
15. **Salt and the brain, in mice.** Mice eating a high-salt diet for three months pumped less blood to their brains and underperformed on memory and behaviour tests. Gut TH17 cells expanded and released IL-17 into the blood. https://www.alzforum.org/news/research-news/gut-immune-cells-not-blood-pressure-blamed-salts-effect-brain

**Months to years: heart, kidney, stomach**
16. **Organ damage can happen without a blood pressure rise.**
    - **Heart:** evidence suggests sodium may directly induce left ventricular hypertrophy, independent of BP. In kidney-disease patients, skin sodium was a strong explanatory variable of left ventricular mass independent of blood pressure or total body water.
    - **Kidney:** the link between urinary sodium and proteinuria persisted after adjustment, suggesting BP-independent effects on glomerular permeability.
    - **Stomach:** pooled data showed higher gastric cancer risk for salty taste preference (aOR 1.59) and always using table salt (aOR 1.33). Excess salt may promote H. pylori colonization.
    - https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6281234_High_salt_intake_a_cause_of_blood_pressure-independent_left_ventricular_hypertrophy, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4448958/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9609108/, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10552-022-01565-y and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7189486/

## Myths and everyday hooks
- **Myth: "Salt is a diuretic."** It is not. One source puts it as "Eating salt causes your body to retain water, not lose it." The extra toilet trips come later, as the kidneys clear the excess. https://scienceinsights.org/is-salt-a-diuretic-or-does-it-cause-water-retention/
- **Myth: "If my blood pressure is normal, I'm fine."** See facts 7 and 16: vessel and organ effects have been seen without a blood pressure change.
- **Myth: "Only the salt shaker matters."** WHO says people are often unaware of the amount of salt they consume. Soy sauce, fish sauce, bouillon, salty snacks and high-sodium sauces are sources. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet
- **Taste adapts, and you can retrain it.**
  - At least 8–10 weeks on a sodium-restricted diet is said to be needed to shift salt taste preference.
  - WCRF notes that our taste for salty foods adapts over time.
  - https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04076280 and https://www.wcrf.org/about-us/news-and-blogs/salt-shaking-up-the-link-with-stomach-cancer/
- **A practical fix.** WHO suggests replacing regular table salt with lower-sodium, potassium-containing substitutes. This is for people not at risk of hyperkalaemia. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/sodium-reduction
- **Salt is disputed.** One source notes some studies found sodium intake below 3 g/day may increase cardiovascular risk and early death. The source is Wikipedia, so check the primary studies before using this. WHO still recommends under 2 g sodium. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_effects_of_salt

- Skin storage and gut-immune findings are mostly animal or early human work. Use "researchers think" or "in mice" wording.
