## What happens when you drink cold water, step by step

**Seconds**

1. **Brain freeze.** Cold on the palate and throat can set off sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia. Pain typically peaks within a few seconds of swallowing the cold food and fades just as quickly once the cold stimulus is gone. The ingestion type tends to resolve within about 10 minutes of removing the cold stimulus.
   - The cause is still debated. One theory is that the cold substance stimulates the sphenopalatine ganglion, a cluster of nerves at the back of the palate.
   - Brain imaging gives a clue. Swallowing ice water causes blood vessels in the brain to dilate, reducing cerebrovascular resistance and increasing blood flow velocity through the middle cerebral artery.
   - Your brain misreads the signal. The trigeminal nerve carries signals from both the mouth and the forehead, so the intense signal is misattributed to the forehead or temples.
   - Quick fix: press your tongue flat against the roof of your mouth.
   - URLs: https://www.sciencefocus.com/the-human-body/brain-freeze, https://scienceinsights.org/what-is-sphenopalatine-ganglioneuralgia-brain-freeze-explained/, https://scienceinsights.org/how-brain-freeze-works-and-how-to-stop-it-fast/ (the last two are secondary; the cerebral-artery finding is attributed to Frontiers in Neurology).

2. **The esophagus briefly stalls.** Cold water slows or abolishes esophageal peristalsis and prolongs the contraction wave in the distal esophagus.
   - In one 1983 case, warm swallows gave normal peristaltic contractions of 74 ± 7 mmHg, while cold swallows gave low-amplitude aperistaltic contractions of 9 ± 1.0 mmHg.
   - In healthy people, failed peristalsis after 3 swallowing exercises suggests peristalsis becomes more impaired as the volume of cold water increases.
   - Researchers describe this as a transient state of relative paralysis in the distal esophagus and lower esophageal sphincter.
   - URLs: https://www.jnmjournal.org/journal/view.html?doi=10.5056%2Fjnm.2014.20.1.79, https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(83)80026-2/pdf, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232722393_Response_of_Esophagus_to_High_and_Low_Temperatures_in_Patients_With_Achalasia

3. **The "chest-pain" angle for achalasia patients.** In a study using 2 °C water, cold water swallow increased lower esophageal sphincter resting pressure and prolonged the duration of esophageal contraction.
   - In 36 achalasia patients, 56% had exacerbated dysphagia or regurgitation when they ate cold food.
   - 88% had relieved chest pain after drinking hot water.
   - A 2025 patient survey found cold items worsened swallowing in 27 patients (60%).
   - URLs: https://www.jnmjournal.org/journal/view.html?uid=45&vmd=Full, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12900736/

**Within about a minute**

4. **"Cold drink heart" (a surprising moment).** Swallowing cold can stimulate the vagus nerve next to the esophagus. A survey of 101 people with atrial fibrillation (afib) found more than half said their afib episodes occurred only after ingesting a cold drink or food, usually within one minute.
   - Swallowing these cold substances may stimulate the esophagus and the nearby vagus nerve, which plays a role in heart rate regulation.
   - About 36% said it was often triggered by something cold after physical activity, like ice-cold water after a workout.
   - It's rare, though. Eating or drinking something cold only rarely always precipitated an episode.
   - Avoiding cold foods and drinks reduced or eliminated afib in 86.4% of patients.
   - URLs: https://www.health.harvard.edu/heart-health/a-cold-drink-may-trigger-an-episode-of-atrial-fibrillation, https://divisionofresearch.kaiserpermanente.org/cold-drinks-trigger-afib/ (study in the Journal of Cardiovascular Electrophysiology, Aug 2025).

5. **Heart rate dips in healthy people too.** Cold- and room-tempered water decreased heart rate and double product (a measure of heart workload) and increased stroke volume; body-tempered water did not. The effect appeared to be mediated by an augmented cardiac vagal tone.
   - A 2025 Frontiers in Physiology study found heart rate decreased immediately after cold water ingestion and stayed suppressed for up to 20 min, despite concurrent sympathetic activation.
   - Caveat: the two commentaries on the first paper disagree on whether this really reduces cardiac workload.
   - URLs: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24684853/, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1627110/xml

**Minutes**

6. **Water reaches your blood very fast.** A study showed ingested water appears in plasma and blood cells as soon as 5 minutes after ingestion (Peronnet et al., 2012). It passes from the intestinal lumen into plasma mainly by passive transport, regulated by osmotic gradients.
   - (Secondary) Peak absorption is typically 20–40 minutes after drinking.
   - URLs: https://www.hydrationforhealth.com/en/hydration-science/hydration-lab/water-and-hydration-physiological-basis-adults/, https://biologyinsights.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-absorb-water-after-drinking/

7. **Cold water gets warmed to body temperature, so it can't "freeze fat".** Experts say cold water will eventually equalize with the body's core temperature, so it's impossible to freeze fat in the stomach.
   - Fat is broken down in the small intestine by bile acids and pancreatic lipase through chemical emulsification.
   - Cold may slow things slightly. Cold water may slightly slow gastric emptying, but it does not solidify fats.
   - URLs: https://verafiles.org/articles/vera-files-fact-check-five-myths-about-drinking-cold-and-war, https://medium.com/health-science/cold-water-vs-warm-water-what-actually-happens-inside-your-body-62a8252216a5, https://www.betterhealthfacts.com/2025/09/drinking-cold-water-after-meals-harm-digestion.html (all secondary). I did not find a WHO, NHS or Mayo page on this myth.

8. **Your mouth tells your brain "thirst solved" before the water is absorbed.** Activation of cold signals from the oropharynx appears to play a role in pre-absorptive water intake signaling. Participants drank significantly less room-temperature water after a previous cold water beverage, showing cold water reduced thirst more effectively.
   - URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0162261 (PLOS ONE).

**About an hour**

9. **The "calorie burn" is real but tiny.** Drinking about 518 ml of 3 °C water caused a small increase in energy expenditure of 4.5% over 60 min.
   - That was well below the theoretical energy cost of warming the water to body temperature.
   - Energy expenditure did not increase after drinking room-temperature distilled water.
   - The study was a response to an earlier claim. Boschmann et al. reported that half a litre of water raised resting energy expenditure by about 30% between 30 and 90 min.
   - A later study put the cost of warming 1 litre from room to body temperature at 15 kcal/1000 ml.
   - URLs: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16822824/, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4735055/, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/287807116_Water-induced_thermogenesis_and_fat_oxidation_a_reassessment

10. **Your kidneys respond.** Absorbed water dilutes the blood. (Secondary) An increase in urine output typically begins approximately 45 to 60 minutes after the initial water consumption.
    - Hormone detail: a 1% decrease of plasma osmolality stimulates ADH secretion, whereas a 10% decrease in blood volume is needed to clearly increase ADH levels.
    - URLs: https://biologyinsights.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-absorb-water-after-drinking/, https://www.hydrationforhealth.com/en/hydration-science/hydration-lab/water-and-hydration-physiological-basis-adults/

**Everyday situations (exercise and heat)**

11. **Cold water slows the rise in core temperature during exercise.** In a 4 °C vs 22 °C trial, the cold water group had a significantly smaller rise in core temperature (0.83°) than the room-temperature group (1.13°).
    - The cold group delayed the increase in core body temperature for at least 30 minutes, while the room-temperature group rose after 15 minutes.
    - Performance in broad jump and time-to-exhaustion tests did not differ.
    - URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22992430/

12. **The effect is small across studies.** A review found cold water ingestion did seem to consistently result in slightly lower (0.1-0.2°C) end-exercise core temperatures.
    - Ice slurry before exercise is stronger: a decrease in core temperature of ~0.5°C consistently arises with cold water or ice slurry ingestion before exercise.
    - URL: https://www.gssiweb.org/docs/default-source/sse-docs/jay_sse_180_final.pdf (Gatorade Sports Science Institute, so an industry source).

13. **Colder isn't always better for rehydration.** In dehydrated athletes, cold water (~5°C) did not induce more voluntary drinking and better hydration status. The maximum came at 16°C water.
    - Volumes drunk (secondary): about 6.4 ml/kg at 16°C, versus 4.2 ml/kg at 5°C and 1.8 ml/kg at 37°C.
    - A likely reason: cold water could prolong satiation by slowing gastric emptying.
    - URLs: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3761501/, https://scienceinsights.org/how-to-quench-thirst-when-water-isnt-enough/

**Days, weeks, months**

14. **No evidence of long-term harm or a cumulative metabolic effect in healthy people.** I found no human trials showing that months of drinking cold water changes organs or metabolism.
    - The only measured cold-water energy effect is the roughly hour-long bump in fact 9. A secondary source estimates it at about 20-30 calories daily (https://www.aihavit.com/blog/en/cold-water-drinking-metabolism-calorie-burn-myth-2026).
    - For a video, say plainly that the "days and months" part is mostly a repeating daily pattern, not a cumulative change.

15. **Habit effect: cold water can make you drink more.** (Secondary) The ACSM recommends cool to cold water (15–22°C) during exercise because it reduces perceived exertion, slows core temperature rise and increases voluntary fluid intake. The hydration benefit comes from drinking more, not from faster absorption. Cold water does not make the small intestine absorb it more efficiently than room-temperature water.
    - URLs: https://mammothmug.com/blogs/hydration/cold-water-vs-warm-water-hydration, https://biologyinsights.com/does-cold-water-hydrate-you-more/

**Myths and cautions**

16. **Cold water causes mucus, colds or heart attacks.** None of this is established.
    - The mucus claim is "not established as a general health effect."
    - A fact-check found that the viral post's claims of heart attack, cancer and digestive harm from cold water were incorrect. The post claimed cold water can cause heart attack, cancer and digestive issues.
    - The fatty-liver myth likely traces to an 11th century book, The Canon of Medicine, by Avicenna.
    - URLs: https://theberkey.com/blogs/water-filter/is-drinking-cold-water-bad-for-you-what-are-the-disadvantages-of-drinking-cold-water, https://verafiles.org/articles/vera-files-fact-check-five-myths-about-drinking-cold-and-war

17. **Who should be careful.** People with migraine are more prone to brain freeze: several studies have found a connection between incidences of brain freeze and a history of migraine. Achalasia and afib patients are covered in facts 3 and 4.
    - URL: https://www.healthline.com/health/sphenopalatine-ganglioneuralgia-brain-freeze

## Gaps in the sources

- I found no WHO, CDC, NHS or Mayo Clinic pages specifically on cold-water physiology, so the strongest sources here are PubMed, PMC and journal papers plus Harvard Health.
- The 1983 esophageal case report (fact 2) is a single patient, and the afib data (fact 4) come from a self-reported survey. Frame both as "can happen in some people", not "happens to everyone".
- Fact 6's 20–40 minute peak, fact 10's urine timing and fact 15 come from secondary or commercial sites. Verify them against a primary physiology source before using exact numbers.
